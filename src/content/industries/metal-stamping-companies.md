## Why stamping shops send welding out

A press floor is built around tonnage, dies and throughput. Welding doesn't fit that rhythm, and it's usually the operation that quietly holds up a shipment. Shops send it out when:

- **The welder is the bottleneck.** The presses are running and the weld cell is three weeks behind.
- **The job is an assembly, not a part.** Two or three stampings that have to become one component, with position and squareness that matter.
- **The material is unforgiving.** Thin-wall stainless, aluminum, titanium or a nickel alloy, where one burned-through weld scraps a part that already has tooling cost in it.
- **The weld has to be certified.** The print flows down a welding specification and there's no certified welder on the floor.
- **The weld will be seen.** A part that gets polished, plated or powder coated shows every flaw in the weld underneath it.
- **The die doesn't exist yet.** The customer needs samples now, and the production tool is eight weeks out.

We take that work as an extension of your shop, not as a competitor. You keep the customer, the program and the margin on the stamping.

## What we weld for stampers

- **Stampings into assemblies.** Two or more pressed parts joined into a single component, fixtured so the critical dimension repeats.
- **Deep drawn shells.** Fittings, flanges, bosses and closures welded to drawn cups and housings, including leak-tight seam welds.
- **Brackets, mounts and tabs** welded to panels, plates and formed sections.
- **Stampings welded to machined or tubular parts.** Mixed assemblies where a pressed part meets a turned fitting, a tube or a machined block. See [weldment fabrication](/services/weldment-fabrication/).
- **Closing and seam welds** on formed housings, enclosures and covers.
- **Rework and salvage.** Short-shipped runs, damaged parts and field returns, welded and blended rather than scrapped.

## Thin gauge is the whole problem

Welding a stamping is not the same job as welding plate. The part is already formed to a dimension, it has residual stress from the press, and the material is often thin enough to burn through if the arc stops for a second too long. Our approach:

- **Fixture first.** Parts are located and clamped so they hold position through welding *and* cooling, not just while the torch is moving.
- **Sequence the welds** so heat is balanced around the part instead of pulling it one direction.
- **Keep heat input low.** [TIG welding](/services/tig-welding/) gives the finest heat control of any manual arc process. On gauge material that's the difference between a part and a reject.
- **Stitch where the design allows it,** rather than running a continuous bead that distorts a panel.
- **Prove it on a first article** before the run, so you approve the real part and not a promise.

## Prototype now, production later

A lot of stamping programs need parts before the die is finished. We weld soft-tooled, laser-cut and hand-formed components so you can ship samples, pass a customer trial or validate fit while the tool is being built. When the die lands and the part goes to volume, the same assembly moves to a fixture and a [production run](/services/production-welding/) with per-part pricing.

## Repeat work, priced per part

For recurring assemblies we build the fixture, weld and inspect a first article for your approval, and run against the approved part from then on. We quote per part, support blanket orders with scheduled releases, and hold the fixture here between releases so there's no setup cost each time.
