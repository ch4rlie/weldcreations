Sanitary and pharmaceutical drawings often call for "passivate" or "electropolish", sometimes both, sometimes interchangeably. The passivation vs electropolishing question comes up on nearly every stainless job with product contact. They're different processes with different purposes and costs. This guide explains what each one does to a stainless surface and how to specify the right one.

## At a glance

| | Passivation | Electropolishing |
|---|---|---|
| **Type of process** | Chemical | Electrochemical |
| **Removes metal?** | Essentially no | Yes, a thin layer |
| **Changes surface finish?** | No | Yes, smoother and brighter |
| **Removes free iron** | Yes | Yes |
| **Leaves a passive surface** | Yes | Yes |
| **Removes heat tint or scale** | No | Only lightly; heavy tint should be removed first |
| **Relative cost** | Lower | Higher |
| **Common specification** | ASTM A967 | ASTM B912 |
| **Typical use** | Food equipment, general stainless fabrication, post-machining cleanup | Pharmaceutical product contact, high-purity, bioprocess, semiconductor |

## What passivation does

Stainless steel resists corrosion because chromium reacts with oxygen to form a thin passive oxide film. Fabrication works against that film: machining, grinding, forming and handling smear free iron onto the surface and leave the chromium-to-iron ratio depleted. Those iron particles rust, and the rust looks like the stainless itself is failing.

Passivation is an acid treatment, usually **citric or nitric acid**, that:

1. **Dissolves free iron and surface contamination.**
2. **Lets the chromium oxide layer re-form** fully across the surface.

It doesn't remove much base metal, doesn't change the roughness meaningfully, and doesn't fix scratches, weld scale or heat tint. The common standard is **ASTM A967** (chemical passivation of stainless parts), with **ASTM A380** covering cleaning and descaling.

Citric acid has largely displaced nitric in many shops: it's effective, less hazardous and easier to handle and dispose of. Some specifications still require nitric, so check your drawing.

## What electropolishing does

Electropolishing is the reverse of plating. The part is the anode in an electrolyte bath, and current removes metal from the surface, fastest at the peaks. The result:

- **A smoother surface.** Peaks are removed faster than valleys, so roughness drops. The improvement depends on the starting finish, and a rough surface will not become mirror-smooth from electropolishing alone.
- **A brighter, reflective appearance.**
- **Deburring of fine burrs** and rounding of sharp micro-edges.
- **A chromium-enriched, passive surface**, because iron dissolves preferentially.
- **Fewer places for product and bacteria to hold on**, which is the reason pharmaceutical specs want it.

It also removes material, typically a few ten-thousandths of an inch. On tight-tolerance parts, that has to be allowed for. Electropolishing will also reveal, rather than hide, underlying problems: weld porosity, inclusions and poor blending become more visible afterwards.

## Which one does your part need?

**Choose passivation when:**
- The part is food equipment, general process equipment, or a machined or fabricated stainless component.
- You need to remove free iron after fabrication and restore corrosion resistance.
- Appearance and roughness are already acceptable.
- Your specification says ASTM A967, or simply "passivate".

**Choose electropolishing when:**
- The part has pharmaceutical, biotech or high-purity product contact.
- The specification sets a surface roughness that mechanical polishing alone won't reliably hit.
- Cleanability is critical: clean-in-place systems, bioburden control, no particle entrapment.
- You want maximum corrosion resistance on product-contact surfaces.
- Your specification says ASTM B912, "EP", or a surface designation that includes electropolishing.

**Both are sometimes specified**, with mechanical polishing to a target roughness, then electropolishing, and sometimes a passivation or passivity verification step afterwards. That's a legitimate sequence; just make sure it's what your spec actually requires, because each step adds cost.

## Welds come first

Neither process is a substitute for proper weld finishing:

- **Heat tint** from welding is a chromium-depleted oxide layer. Passivation doesn't remove it. It must be removed mechanically or by pickling, and only then passivated.
- **Weld scale and spatter** must be cleaned off before either process.
- **Crevices, undercut and lap joints** stay crevices. No surface treatment makes a badly designed joint cleanable.

This is why sanitary fabrication starts with full-penetration, back-purged welds and proper blending. ## How to specify it on the drawing

Vague finishing callouts cause rework. A good callout states:

1. **The process and standard.** For example "Passivate per ASTM A967, citric", or "Electropolish per ASTM B912".
2. **Which surfaces.** Product contact only, or all over.
3. **The surface roughness**, with the target Ra and whether it applies before or after electropolishing.
4. **Weld treatment.** Blend welds flush, remove heat tint.
5. **Verification and documentation.** The test method and the certificates you want delivered with the parts.
